2.1 - How is pitch generated?

With stringed instruments such as a guitars, violins or even pianos, sound is generated by standing waves. A standing wave is a transverse wave where any given particle has zero net displacement and moves perpendicular to the source. In these instruments, the standing wave moves through the string and the resultant changes in air pressure are perceived by our ears as sound, with a discernable pitch and timbre.

Depiction of the proportional relationships between amplitude, displacement and pressure disturbance that creates waves. (Flipping Physics, n.d.)


For a stretched string, the fundamental frequency may be derived from the one-dimensional wave equation, and is given by:

\begin{equation} v_f = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \end{equation}

Where:

  • \( v_f \) is the fundamental [main] frequency in Hertz
  • \( L \) is the scale length of the guitar in Meters
  • \( T \) is the tension of the string in Newtons
  • \( \mu \) is the mass per unit length of the string in Kilograms per Meter

(Grimes, 2014)


2.2 - Sound physics in a guitar

For this section in our investigation, we decided to verify the formula used in calculations involving string tension and frequency.


Let's take the example of the A string on a guitar. Tuning systems are often defined by A = 'X' Hz, where western music is typically based on A = 440 Hz, however due to the nature of octaves, and the way the guitar is strung, the 'Low A String' on a standard range guitar is 110 Hz.

\(\therefore\) \(v_f=110~Hz\)


We also know that the standard scale length of a guitar (the distance from the nut to saddle and length of vibrating element) is commonly 25.5 (in).

\(\therefore\) \(L\approx 0.64770~m\)


Through an archived D'Addario specifications document for their strings, we were able to determine the linear density of an A string from the 'EXL110 string pack' to be 0.00023964 (lb/in)

(D'Addario is a well established guitar products manufacturer).

\(\therefore\) \(\mu\approx 0.0042795~kg~m^{-1}\)


We can now substitute these values into the formula:

\begin{equation} 110 = \frac{1}{2\times 0.64770}\sqrt{\frac{T}{0.0042795}} \end{equation}

Transposing to solve for \(T\) we get:

\begin{equation} T = (110 \times 1.2954)^{2} \times 0.0042795 \\ \\ \therefore T \approx 86.89~N \end{equation}

This aligns with found experimental results from an Illinois university student study which found the tension of the A string to be 85.57 Newtons (Achillies, 2000).

We cautiously chose to use values obtained from identical models of string/guitar in order to establish validity for our analysis. Fluctuations in tension between both similar and different string models can also be caused by the differences in the metal coating, its thickness and a variety of environmental factors outside the scope of our investigation.
